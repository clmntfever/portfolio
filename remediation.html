The Problem

Security teams were drowning in data,
but starving for direction.

Panaseer is a continuous controls monitoring platform used by enterprises. It gave CISO-level teams amazing data. But Security teams logged data from hundreds of tools and Panaseer to have to surface this in a human way.

But surfacing wasn't enough. Security teams could identify risks, but lacked a structured way to track, assign, and resolve them at scale. Every morning meant updating spreadsheets and manually coordinating remediation efforts.

"We have 4,000 control failures. My team can action maybe 40 of them. All I'm asking: where do we start?"

Discovery

before designing for it.

I facilitated a half-day user co-workshop with 10 stakeholders: product, engineering, customer success, and end users to align on the problem definition before any design work started. The generated insights allowed us to narrow our design space significantly.

The Process

From Crazy 8s to shipped
product in 6 months.

Three phases, each validated before moving forward. Click any card to expand.

01

FigJam Discovery

Mapped the full remediation workflow with the team. Identified with the data and engineering team the properties and data input/output of a remediation objective. The journey mapped two fundamental modes: creation (rare, deliberate) and monitoring (frequent, quick-scan). This split shaped the entire IA.

02

Crazy 8s

8 concepts / 8 minutes / 3 perspectives: engineering, customer success, and end users.

3 perspectives:

💻

Engineers — Focused on data structure

Remediation objects needed to connect to control gaps, assets, and users at the same time. That's a complex relationship that shaped the entire information architecture. Most designers skip this conversation.

🤝

Customer Success — Focused on client feedback

Clients weren't asking for a better dashboard. They were asking: "Can I assign this to my engineer and know they've seen it?" That's an ownership problem, not a visualisation problem.

🔍

End Users — Focused on analyst expectations

Analysts think in deadlines and ownership, not risk scores. They wanted something closer to a ticket system — familiar, actionable, not another security tool.

03

Wireframes

Low-fidelity flows for Create, List, and Detail screens. Validated with engineers early. I built low-fi wireframes to test the core flows with real users. These were deliberately rough — the goal was to validate structure, not visual design.

The Solution

Create, track, and close remediation objectives in one streamlined workflow.

The Remediation Objectives feature gives Security teams a structured, end-to-end workspace for managing their remediation work. Two views exist: an overview page surfacing the most important information at a glance, and an objective-level detail view, with a timeline and checklist to track overall remediation progress, to track control gaps within the expected timeframe.

Reflection

Things this case study taught me about product design.

01

The problem acts as the first design tool

I wasn't designing a feature. I was redesigning a process that existed in spreadsheets, email threads, and tribal knowledge. Getting that framing right early — through discovery — kept the design focused on real behaviour, not ideal workflows.

02

Customer Success knows what User Research often misses

Pulling Customer Success into the Crazy 8s session shifted our entire direction. They brought patterns from real client conversations that no interview script would have surfaced. Cross-functional design is faster and truer.

03

Earned simplicity ships better than imposed simplicity

Every time I wanted to simplify the UI, the underlying data model pushed back. The final design is simple because we mapped the complexity early, not because we ignored it. Simple interfaces are usually complex under the hood.
